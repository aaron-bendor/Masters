\section{Project Management and Reflection}\label{sec:reflection}

This section addresses the \emph{Project Management} and \emph{Reflection}
learning outcomes of the module assessment criteria
\cite{markscheme}.

\subsection{Project structure and milestone management}
\label{sec:reflect-pm}

The project was scoped at the outset as a three-tier validation
pipeline (synthetic, simulated, real-world) extending a single source
paper \cite{morimura2013}. This top-down decomposition served well as a
stable organising structure: each tier had clear inputs, outputs, and
acceptance criteria, and the natural sequencing (upstream
validation locks before downstream tiers depend on it) gave a clean
de-risking order.

Internal milestone management was driven by a running
\code{research\_logbook.md} file that holds twenty
``iter-$N$'' entries spanning the full project. Each iter
documents one experimental cycle: hypothesis, setup, result, honest
read, and next action. Two practices that worked particularly well:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The auto-memory file as an \emph{epistemic} index.}
A separate file (\code{MEMORY.md} with links to per-finding markdown
records) was used for ``things I now believe and the evidence for them''
as opposed to ``things I did and when''. Concretely, the file
records statements such as ``empirical $g$ on SUMO is sparse to the
point of being uninformative for the optimiser'' with a pointer to the
specific iter that established it. This separation made it possible to
quickly distinguish ``a working assumption that was right when I made
it but might now be stale'' from ``a finding I have evidence for''.
\item \textbf{Audit-before-extend.} A guiding rule, learned from
iter-9's retraction of an earlier headline number, was to audit
the upstream implementation before extending it. Two specific audits
saved time: (i) the gradient finite-difference check at $\sim 10^{-9}$
relative error confirmed the analytic gradient derivation before any
sweep was run on features, avoiding the trap of attributing fit
behaviour to a buggy derivative; (ii) the iter-18 contrast-correlation
diagnostic established that AUC alone is an unreliable signal of
chain quality, which materially altered how the Phase~4 result was
framed.
\end{itemize}

Two practices that did not work as well are worth recording explicitly:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Underestimating the compute scaling of the $f+g$ loss.}
The first $f+g$ sweep at SUMO scale was projected at $\sim 1$ hour and
killed at $9.5$ hours. The retry at smaller $|X_o|$ and
$\code{maxiter}$ was projected at $\sim 30$ minutes and killed at
$3$~hours. Two iterations were burned before the structural
diagnosis (the hitting-rate gradient is $O(|X_o| \cdot n^3)$ and the
$84\%$-floor loss landscape is degenerate at this scale) was reached.
In future I would profile a single iteration before committing to a
sweep at unfamiliar configuration.
\item \textbf{Late identification of the OD-mix confound.} The Tier-3
rush-vs-off-peak proxy was scoped before the methodological cost of
the OD-mix confound was clearly understood. The OD-rebalancing
extension --- the methodologically correct fix --- was added in the
final week, and as of writing the matched-pool experiment is still
in flight. Earlier scoping would have caught this; the lesson is that
``proxy weakness'' should be a structured pre-experiment audit item,
not an ad-hoc discussion item.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Benchmarking against the example dissertations}
\label{sec:reflect-bench}

I read three example MEng Design Engineering dissertations from
previous years to calibrate quality expectations. The benchmarks I
took from each:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Aditya Munot, ``Optimal Transport for Complex Sharing
Economies with QoS Guarantees''}: the notation table at section~1
that gives readers a clean reference for the math. I adopted this
convention in \cref{sec:notation}. The clean separation of theoretical
framework, scenario application, and benchmarking-against-state-of-the-art
also informed how I structured the validation tiers.
\item \textbf{Nora Luo, [thesis title placeholder]}: the discipline
of foregrounding limitations in the discussion rather than burying
them, and of writing the conclusion as a quantified claim
($X = Y$ \% within tolerance $Z$) rather than a narrative. I have
followed this style in \cref{sec:discussion} where every claim is
backed by a number.
\item \textbf{Antanas Zilinskas, [thesis title placeholder]}: the
use of figure-paired tables (e.g.\ a small numerical summary alongside
a curve) rather than relying on either alone. I have not yet adopted
this fully but the figure scaffolding is in place for the eventual
revision.
\end{itemize}

By the assessment criteria of \cite{markscheme}, the strengths I would
foreground are: contextual investigation (a critical engagement with
three real-world AVI datasets, only one of which is actually usable
for per-trajectory work, and an explicit positioning against the
source paper and rerouting-detection prior art); validation
(three independent validation tiers, each with its own honest caveats
and a quantified ceiling); and reflection (this section, and the
honest framing of the $f+g$ infeasibility, plus the basin-selection
question chased to resolution on both tiers via multistart rather than
left as a hand-wave). The weaknesses are: the single day-pool on Tier~3
(no multi-pool replication, in contrast to the five-seed Tier-2
confirmation), and that the headline detection-AUC lift from features
proved seed-dependent rather than robust once the sweep was run.

\subsection{Personal skill development}
\label{sec:reflect-skills}

The technical skills I leaned on heavily and improved in: deriving and
finite-difference-verifying analytic gradients (Eqs.~12, 15, 17--18 of
the source paper); writing software with a clean separation between
the inverter's mathematical core (in \code{morimura.py}, kept faithful
to the paper) and the application-specific loaders that surround it
(\code{sumo\_to\_phase3.py}, \code{real\_data/load\_xuancheng.py});
and writing a logbook that records both the successful and the
unsuccessful experimental cycles in enough detail that I could
critique my own past decisions a month later.

The non-technical skill I would name as the one that most shaped the
project's outcome is \emph{honest framing of negative results}. The
project did not produce the headline ``real-world detector with a
high AUC and clean chain recovery'' that the SUMO Tier-2 result would
have predicted. Instead it produced: a controlled-validation result
($\sim 0.71$ AUC, $69\%$ gap closure on labelled SUMO), an
empirically-verified data ceiling (the $2$-step refutation closes a
tempting line of work), and an honest real-world result
($\sim 0.53$ AUC on a behavioural proxy without ground-truth labels)
that quantifies the gap between simulated and deployed
detectability. Each of these is publishable, in part because they
were framed honestly --- but the temptation in week~30 of the project
to switch the real-world contrast to a stronger but methodologically
incorrect proxy (holiday-vs-normal) had to be resisted. Resisting that
temptation in real time, against the prospect of a higher headline
number, is the skill I most want to carry forward.
