\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Context and research gap}
\label{sec:context}\label{sec:background}

Satellite-navigation and live-traffic services have become part of the
route-choice system rather than passive map displays. Travel information
changes route-choice behaviour in congested networks
\cite{yang2001atis,ramos2020information}, and routing-app shortest-path
guidance can redistribute traffic in ways that affect wider network
performance and residential cut-through traffic
\cite{thai2016,cabannes2018gps,krichene2018}. This creates a measurement
problem. Traffic counts and trajectories no longer necessarily reveal
only the preferences of unaided drivers; they may also encode the
recommendations of external routing systems reacting to congestion and to
other guided vehicles.

This matters because route-choice inference, demand estimation, pricing
and infrastructure appraisal rely on revealed-preference data. If a
substantial fraction of observed trips is externally guided, downstream
models may mistake recommender-induced behaviour for intrinsic driver
preference. The problem addressed here is therefore detection: given only
sparse observation of a road network, can we distinguish trajectories
that look intrinsic from trajectories that look externally influenced?

\subsection{Related work and novelty}
\label{sec:inverse-mc}\label{sec:route-softmax}\label{sec:partial-route-prior}\label{sec:rerouting-prior}

Route choice is often represented as a sequence of link-level decisions.
Multinomial-logit and recursive-logit models parameterise the probability
of taking the next road edge from the current edge
\cite{ben-akiva1985,frejinger2007,fosgerau2013route,mai2015nested}. In a
one-step Markov-chain (MC) form, with road edges as states, the transition
probability is
\begin{equation}
\pT(x'\mid x) =
\frac{\exp(s(x,x'))}{\sum_{x'' \in \mathrm{adj}(x)} \exp(s(x,x''))},
\label{eq:softmax}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{adj}(x)$ is the set of legal successors of edge $x$ and
$s(x,x')$ is a feature-based score. Related work also estimates
route-choice models from incomplete trips, sparse sensor observations or
inverse preference learning \cite{vanoijen2020sensor,mai2023incomplete,ziebart2008maxent}.
Those papers address important adjacent estimation problems, but they
are normally framed as single-behavioural-model estimation rather than
two-regime trajectory detection.

Morimura, Osogami and Id\'e introduced an inverse Markov-chain method for
recovering a softmax-parametric chain from partial observations
\cite{morimura2013}. Their method fits a chain using counts at a subset
of observed states $\Xo$ and, optionally, hitting rates between observed
states. It is the estimator used in this dissertation. The contribution
here is not a new inverse-chain estimator. It is a two-regime
partial-observation filter that fits one chain per regime, scores each
trajectory with a log-likelihood ratio and reports diagnostics that
separate transition-level chain recovery from aggregate confounds such as
origin-destination (OD) mix, optimiser basins and model-order
misspecification.

\subsection{Filter specification and assumptions}
\label{sec:intro-filter}\label{sec:methodology}\label{sec:filter-design}
\label{sec:morimura}\label{sec:morimura-formulation}\label{sec:loss-gradients}

The filter takes a directed road graph $\mathcal{X}$, an observed subset
$\Xo \subseteq \mathcal{X}$, regime-labelled station counts
$\fobs_A,\fobs_B$ and held-out trajectories. For each regime it fits a
softmax-parametric MC by minimising Morimura et al.'s regularised
log-ratio loss. The fitted chains $\hat{P}_A$ and $\hat{P}_B$ then define
a log-likelihood-ratio (LLR) score
\[
\Lam(\tau)=\sum_t \left[\log \hat{p}_B(x_{t+1}\mid x_t)
-\log \hat{p}_A(x_{t+1}\mid x_t)\right],
\]
and validation reports the area under the receiver-operating-characteristic
curve (AUC) over possible decision thresholds.

The Morimura estimator carries assumptions that are used later in the
report. First, it assumes a one-step MC: the next edge depends on the
current edge, not the full previous path. Second, its count term treats
$\fobs$ as a sample from a stationary distribution, and its optional
hitting-rate term treats $\gobs$ as stationary first-passage evidence.
Third, the softmax form restricts recoverable behaviour to the chosen
feature and parameter family. In the SUMO and Xuancheng tiers the data
come from finite, time-varying traffic windows, so the fitted chain is a
time-aggregated surrogate for that window rather than behavioural ground
truth. The empirical full-observation transition matrix used for
comparison is also a finite-data reference, not an oracle.

\paragraph{Diagnostics.}\label{sec:detector}
The scalar AUC is reported with transition-level diagnostics. At
branching states, the fitted regime contrast
$\Delta_{\mathrm{fit}}=\hat{P}_B-\hat{P}_A$ is compared with the
full-observation empirical contrast $\Delta_{\mathrm{emp}}$ using stacked
Pearson correlation and per-state cosine similarity. A high AUC with a
near-zero transition contrast indicates aggregate-bias-driven detection,
whereas positive contrast correlation supports a chain-recovery claim.

\paragraph{Extensions.}\label{sec:features}\label{sec:2step-diag}\label{sec:od-rebal}
Three extensions are evaluated in the tiers. The feature-regularised
variant adds road class, speed, lane and turn-geometry features as shared
parameters. The empirical two-step test checks whether
$(x_{t-1},x_t)$-conditional routing memory improves over the one-step
reference before a parametric two-step inverse-MC model is attempted.
The OD-rebalancing step matches real-world regime pools by
$(\mathrm{origin\ zone},\mathrm{destination\ zone})$ before comparing
rush-hour and off-peak routing.

\paragraph{Validation split.}
Test trajectories are withheld from the empirical transition matrix and
from the empirical-reference LLR scorer. The inverse-MC fit uses aggregate
station counts for the whole regime window, not counts recomputed after
removing held-out vehicles. The reported AUCs are therefore held out at
the trajectory-scoring level, not a fully out-of-sample sensor-count
experiment.

\subsection{Experimental structure}
\label{sec:roadmap}\label{sec:sumo}\label{sec:avi-prior}

The report follows the experimental tiers directly. Tier~1 validates the
inverse-MC estimator against Morimura et al.'s synthetic benchmark.
Tier~2 uses Simulation of Urban MObility (SUMO) to test the full filter
under labelled sat-navigation rerouting. Tier~3 applies the same filter
to automatic vehicle identification (AVI) data from Xuancheng, China,
where rush-hour versus off-peak travel is used as an unlabelled proxy for
external routing influence. The Discussion compares what generalises
across tiers, the Conclusion states the supported claims, and the final
section covers project management and reflection.
