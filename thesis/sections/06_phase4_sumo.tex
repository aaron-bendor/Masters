\section{Tier 2: Controlled validation of the filter in SUMO}
\label{sec:phase4-sumo}

This tier is the controlled, end-to-end validation of the
partial-observation routing-influence filter (Algorithm~\ref{alg:filter})
in the only setting that supplies ground-truth regime labels: an
identical population of vehicles is simulated once with sat-navigation
rerouting turned off and once with it turned on. Every component of the
filter is exercised at city-block scale ($n = 2{,}405$ edges): the
inverse-MC estimator is fitted per regime, the log-likelihood-ratio
scorer is applied to held-out trajectories, the feature-regularised
filter variant is tested against a no-features baseline, and the
filter's diagnostic suite (per-row Pearson/cosine, multistart basin
check, empirical $2$-step memory audit) is run on the resulting fits.
A trajectory-length sweep traces how the scoring window $T$ shifts both
the empirical reference and the fitted detector together, and we close
with a documented infeasibility of the hitting-rate ($f+g$) extension at
this scale --- a design-relevant failure mode for the filter's optional
$\gobs$ input.

A note on terminology used throughout this section. We refer to the
no-rerouting regime as ``static unguided routing'': SUMO routes those
vehicles by static shortest-path Dijkstra with no live rerouting. This is
a controlled proxy for intrinsic route choice, not a behavioural model of
human preference, and we read the fitted ``intrinsic'' chain accordingly.
We also use ``empirical $1$-step reference'' (rather than an
information-theoretic ceiling) for the AUC obtained from the
full-observation Laplace-smoothed transition matrix; it is the best a
$1$-step detector can do \emph{given this finite data and smoothing}, and
finite-sample and train/test variation can move it (and occasionally
produce apparent anomalies, \cref{sec:phase5-multistart}).

\subsection{Scenario construction}\label{sec:phase4-scenario}

A square-kilometre bounding box of central Munich (the ``bigger-bbox''
network, $\sim 3.7\,\text{km} \times 3.3\,\text{km}$, derived from OSM via
\code{netconvert}) yields $n = 2{,}405$ directed edges after filtering
to the largest strongly-connected passenger-allowed component, with
$E = 5{,}987$ directed transitions and mean out-degree $\sim 2.5$. A
demand of $\sim 28{,}800$ vehicles is generated by
\code{randomTrips.py} over a four-hour horizon at $-p\,0.5$. Each vehicle
is replayed twice under the same insertion times and origin/destination
pairs:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Intrinsic regime:} \code{--device.rerouting.probability 0}.
Vehicles route via static shortest-path Dijkstra and never react to
congestion.
\item \textbf{Sat-navigation regime:} \code{--device.rerouting.probability 1}.
Every vehicle re-evaluates its route every $30\,\mathrm{s}$ on the basis
of observed live edge travel times.
\end{itemize}
Operating at $-p\,0.5$ was confirmed in iter-9 of the project to be
the cleanest demand point: at higher demand both regimes saturate and
the sat-navigation contrast collapses; at lower demand there is
insufficient congestion to drive any rerouting. At $-p\,0.5$ the
satnav regime delivers $\sim 36\%$ more trips than intrinsic with
matched insertion ($28{,}098$ vs.\ $20{,}700$ finishing).

For each regime we expose vehicle counts \code{edgedata.*.xml} (the
empirical $\fobs$) and complete per-vehicle routes \code{vehroutes.*.xml}
(used as the test set and to construct the empirical chain for the
contrast diagnostic). The inverter sees only $|X_o| = 601$ randomly
sampled edges --- $25\%$ of the network --- consistent with the
operating point of the Morimura $\S 6.1$ benchmark.

A stationarity caveat applies here and carries through to Tier~3. The
Morimura observation model assumes the counts $\fobs$ are samples of a
\emph{stationary} distribution and that $\gobs$ are stationary hitting
rates. SUMO produces finite-horizon, dynamic, congestion-affected
counts over a four-hour window, so the \code{edgedata} totals are best
read as empirical occupancy / visit-frequency summaries rather than
exact stationary samples. We treat them as such: the inverse fit is a
controlled operational test of the pipeline under a stationarity
approximation, not a claim that the fitted chain is behavioural ground
truth. This is one reason we report the full-observation empirical
transition matrix as a finite-data reference rather than as a true
ceiling, and why the per-row contrast diagnostic
(\cref{sec:detector}) --- which compares fitted and empirical
\emph{contrasts} on the same finite data --- is the load-bearing
evidence rather than the absolute AUC.

Three findings. First, the aggregate detection lift is \emph{not}
statistically robust: the paired $\AUC_{\mathrm{fit}}$ lift averages
$+0.024 \pm 0.026$ across the five seeds, positive on four but reversing
on seed~$101$ (a paired $t$-test gives $t \approx 2.0$, $p \approx 0.11$,
not significant at $\alpha = 0.05$). The single-seed $+0.032$ headline of
\cref{sec:phase4-features-result} is therefore a favourable draw, not a
replicable property of the aggregate AUC.

Second --- and this is the load-bearing result --- the per-row
chain-recovery metrics \emph{are} robust. The stacked Pearson lift
averages $+0.050 \pm 0.023$ and is positive on \emph{all five} seeds
($t \approx 4.9$, $p \approx 0.008$); the per-row cosine median lifts on
all five as well (mean $\approx +0.25$). Seed~$101$ is the cleanest
illustration of the AUC$\leftrightarrow$chain-quality decoupling first
noted in iter-18: features \emph{hurt} its detection AUC
($0.581 \to 0.559$) while \emph{improving} its chain recovery (cosine
median $0.062 \to 0.475$). The honest framing of the features lever is
therefore that it is a \textbf{chain-recovery} improvement, not an
aggregate-detection improvement.

Third, the \code{features=none} fit reproduces the bimodal gap-closure
structure observed independently in iter-16: seeds $\{7, 23\}$ cluster
low ($30$--$34\%$) and seeds $\{42, 101, 2024\}$ cluster high
($61$--$62\%$), with nothing in between. The same seeds fall in the same
clusters as the earlier run, indicating the split is a stable property of
the L-BFGS basin structure on these instances rather than measurement
noise. The \code{features=none} $\AUC_{\mathrm{fit}}$ across seeds is
$0.575 \pm 0.027$, matching the historical $\pm 0.024$ band. Whether this
low basin is escapable by multi-start re-initialisation --- left open by the
multi-seed sweep --- is settled in \cref{sec:phase4-multistart}: it is not.

\subsection{Multistart basin check: the low basin is not an initialisation artefact}
\label{sec:phase4-multistart}

The bimodal basin structure of \cref{sec:phase4-multiseed} raises an
optimiser-quality question: is the low-gap basin on seeds $\{7, 23\}$ an
artefact of the zero-initialisation that L-BFGS starts from, escapable by
multi-start re-initialisation? To test this we re-ran both low-basin seeds
with $K = 5$ random restarts per chain (initial $\theta_0 \sim
\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, \code{features=none}, $T = 20$), selecting the restart
with the highest held-out validation log-likelihood and comparing it to the
zero-init baseline (\cref{fig:phase4-multistart}).

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.98\linewidth]{fig06_phase4_multistart.png}
\caption{Tier~2 multistart basin check on the two low-basin seeds
(\code{features=none}, $K = 5$, $T = 20$). \textbf{(a)} The val-selected
multistart detector ties the zero-init baseline on seed~$7$ and is markedly
worse on seed~$23$; neither approaches the $\sim 61\%$ good-basin level of
seeds $\{42, 101, 2024\}$ (dashed). \textbf{(b)} Every one of the ten random
restarts (five per regime) converges to a strictly \emph{higher} final loss
than zero-init --- so zero-init is the best optimum found, and the low basin
is not an initialisation artefact.}
\label{fig:phase4-multistart}
\end{figure}

The answer is negative, and informatively so. On \emph{both} seeds, every one
of the ten random restarts (five per regime) converged to a strictly
\emph{worse} training loss \emph{and} a worse held-out validation
log-likelihood than the zero-init baseline (\cref{fig:phase4-multistart}b):
on seed~$7$ the restarts reach intrinsic-chain loss $44.99$--$45.5$ against
the baseline's $42.15$; on seed~$23$, $457$--$459$ against $455.9$. The
val-selected multistart detector therefore \emph{ties} the baseline on
seed~$7$ ($30.2\%$ vs $30.3\%$ gap closed) and is markedly \emph{worse} on
seed~$23$ ($7.9\%$ vs $34.2\%$); neither approaches the $\sim 61\%$
good-basin level of seeds $\{42, 101, 2024\}$ (\cref{fig:phase4-multistart}a).

Two conclusions follow. First, the cross-seed bimodality is \emph{not} an
initialisation artefact: zero-init is the best optimum found on these
instances and random restarts cannot reach the high basin, so the low gap on
seeds $\{7, 23\}$ is a property of those instances' loss landscapes rather
than of where the optimiser starts. This closes the open question left by
\cref{sec:phase4-multiseed} and reinforces the reading that the split is
structural. Second, the seed-$23$ collapse to $7.9\%$ is itself a small
cautionary result: selecting the fit by held-out \emph{log-likelihood} picked
a chain that \emph{discriminates worse} (the val-selected sat-nav restart was
among the lowest-AUC of the five), another instance of the
likelihood$\leftrightarrow$detection decoupling that recurs throughout this
work (\cref{sec:phase4-features-result}). The honest caveat is that all fits
were \code{maxiter}-capped at $1{,}500$ iterations and the random inits have
large norm ($\lVert\theta_0\rVert \approx 92$ at $d = 8{,}392$); we have thus
shown that the \emph{standard} random-restart recipe does not escape the low
basin, not that the basin is provably global.

\subsection{Higher-order memory diagnostic: a regularised 2-step
empirical lifts the reference}
\label{sec:phase4-2step}

A natural hypothesis for the still-flipped rows is that the
$1$-step Markov assumption is empirically violated: real drivers'
next-edge choice depends on \emph{both} the current and previous edge
(``do not U-turn'', ``keep on the arterial''), and the $1$-step softmax
cannot encode that dependence. If true, an empirical $2$-step detector
that uses $(x_{t-1}, x_t)$ as context should out-discriminate the
$1$-step empirical. The question is whether any reasonably-regularised
$2$-step empirical can lift the reference, given that the raw
$(x_{t-1}, x_t)$ pair space is huge ($|X|^2 \approx 5.8\text{M}$ at the
bigger-bbox scale) and most of it is empty.

We test two regularisation schemes across the same five SUMO seeds
used in \cref{sec:phase4-multiseed} (training/test split unchanged):

\paragraph{Dirichlet smoothing (toward uniform-over-neighbors).}
$\widehat{P}_{2,\alpha}(y | x_p, x) = (c(x_p, x, y) + \alpha) /
(c(x_p, x) + \alpha \cdot |\mathrm{adj}(x)|)$,
swept over $\alpha \in \{10^{-3}, 10^{-2}, 10^{-1}, 1, 10, 100\}$.

\paragraph{Interpolation backoff (toward 1-step empirical).}
$\widehat{P}_{\mathrm{back}}(y | x_p, x) =
\lambda \cdot \widehat{P}_{2}^{\mathrm{MLE}}(y | x_p, x) +
(1 - \lambda) \cdot \widehat{P}_{1}(y | x)$,
where $\widehat{P}_{2}^{\mathrm{MLE}}$ is the unsmoothed 2-step MLE
and $\widehat{P}_{1}$ is the 1-step empirical chain that elsewhere
serves as the full-observation reference. Two $\lambda$ modes:
fixed $\lambda \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9, 1.0\}$ and
count-conditional
$\lambda = N(x_p, x) / (N(x_p, x) + \kappa)$ with
$\kappa \in \{1, 10, 100, 1000\}$.
The count-conditional mode trusts the 2-step MLE in proportion to its
observation support, falling back smoothly to the 1-step empirical for
rare contexts.

Three findings (\cref{tab:phase4-2step}).

First, the apparent \emph{negative} result from a single-seed Dirichlet
analysis ($0.666 < 0.685$ at seed 42, $\Delta = -0.019$) is driven by
two compounding errors. Seed 42 has the highest 1-step AUC of the five
SUMO instances ($0.685$ vs.\ mean $0.652$), leaving the least room for
any 2-step lift; multi-seed averaging exposes that the seed-42 ceiling
masked. Separately, Dirichlet shrinks the 2-step estimate toward
uniform-over-neighbors, which discards the marginal routing information
that the 1-step empirical already encodes. The natural shrinkage target
is the 1-step empirical itself, not uniform.

Second, interpolation backoff toward the 1-step empirical resolves both
issues. The count-conditional weight
$\lambda(x_p, x) = N(x_p, x) / (N(x_p, x) + \kappa)$
trusts the 2-step MLE proportionally to its observation support; at
$\kappa = 100$ the per-seed $\Delta$ vector is
$\{+0.090, +0.094, +0.099, +0.107, +0.110\}$ (s42, s23, s2024, s7, s101),
mean $+0.100$, standard deviation $0.009$ --- every seed wins, and the
lift is tighter than any other multi-seed effect in this section
(\cref{sec:phase4-multiseed}'s features Pearson lift was
$+0.050 \pm 0.023$ on the per-row metric; the aggregate AUC lift
$+0.024 \pm 0.026$ reversed on seed~$101$). At $\kappa = 100$ the
effective $\lambda$ across the $3{,}528$ observed $(x_p, x)$ pairs on
seed 42 has median $0.21$, 10th--90th percentiles $[0.03, 0.81]$: the
detector mostly relies on the 1-step baseline and only trusts the
2-step part where strong observation support exists.

Third, the lift is not knife-edge in the hyperparameter. The
fixed-$\lambda$ sweep gives $\Delta \in \{+0.088, +0.092, +0.094, +0.095\}$
for $\lambda \in \{0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.9\}$, all positive across all 5
seeds. The count-conditional sweep gives
$\Delta \in \{+0.095, +0.097, +0.100, +0.097\}$ for
$\kappa \in \{1, 10, 100, 1000\}$. The hyperparameter selection
caveat applies in the narrow technical sense ($\kappa = 100$ was the
sweep maximum on the same test set used for AUC, so the exact $+0.100$
is mildly test-tuned) but the qualitative conclusion ---
\emph{any reasonable backoff strength lifts AUC by $\sim +0.09$ to
$+0.10$ across all five SUMO seeds} --- is robust to selection.

\paragraph{Reading.} A properly regularised 2-step empirical
\emph{does} lift the reference, decisively, across every SUMO seed
tested: the regularised 2-step AUC reaches $0.752 \pm 0.015$ against
the 1-step baseline of $0.652 \pm 0.022$, a $+0.100$ delta whose
per-seed signs all point the same way. Two implications follow.

The per-row chain-recovery gap left by the $1$-step features lever
(\cref{sec:phase4-features-result}: $+0.215$ stacked Pearson on
\texttt{[all branching]}, $\sim 45\%$ of rows still with
wrong-direction contrast) is consistent with the $1$-step softmax
family being structurally unable to express $(x_{t-1}, x_t)$-conditional
choice. This is the regime where a parametric 2-step Morimura
extension is the right design lever, with the regularised empirical
sitting roughly $+0.10$ AUC above the 1-step ceiling at $T = 20$ as the
performance target it would need to approach.

We do \emph{not} report an $\AUC_{\mathrm{fit}}$ for the regularised
2-step here. A parametric 2-step Morimura inverter is a non-trivial
extension --- a different state space, different gradient, different
ceiling argument --- and is left to future work
(\cref{sec:disc-future}). The empirical result above establishes that
the signal exists and bounds the achievable 2-step inverter
performance from above; what it does not do is settle whether the
parametric inversion route can recover that bound under partial
observation.

\subsection{Trajectory-length sweep: lifting the ceiling and the detector together}
\label{sec:phase4-tsweep}

The $0.685$ ceiling is specific to the chosen test trajectory length
$T = 20$. The log-likelihood-ratio score $\Lam(\tau)$ is a sum of $T$
independent log-ratio increments, so its variance scales as $\sim T$.
Longer trajectories produce a tighter score distribution between the
two regimes and a higher achievable AUC; the data is ``the same'',
only the observation window is wider.

Two findings worth recording. First, the empirical ceiling rises from
$0.685$ to $0.801$ between $T=20$ and $T=40$, matching an earlier

the ceiling at $T=20$ was an artefact of the truncation window, not a
fundamental data limit. Second, the per-row Pearson is constant across
$T$ at $\sim +0.21$. $T$ is purely a scoring-window lever: it lets the
trajectory-level score discriminate more sharply, but it does not
change the chain that was fit. The fitted chain captures the same
$\sim 21\%$ of the true per-row contrast regardless of how long we
observe each test trip.

The strongest single-seed SUMO headline is therefore $\AUC = 0.708$
at $T=40$, $69\%$ of the gap closed against the full-observation
empirical $1$-step reference of $0.801$, with a stable per-row chain
Pearson of $+0.216$ on \texttt{[all branching]}.

\subsection{Disentangling sat-nav adoption from congestion-induced rerouting}
\label{sec:phase4-rho-demand}

A methodological concern arises in advance of Tier~3
(\cref{sec:phase5-xuancheng}), where no ground-truth sat-navigation
label exists and we will rely on a behavioural rush-vs-off-peak proxy.
Rush hour and off-peak differ in \emph{two} ways simultaneously: in
sat-navigation adoption rate (presumably higher when journeys are time
critical) and in congestion level (heavy demand changes routing
incentives for \emph{all} drivers, sat-nav users and unaided drivers
alike). Real data cannot separate the two contributions, because there
is no Xuancheng configuration in which the congestion level is varied
while the sat-navigation adoption rate is held fixed (or vice versa).
SUMO can: the per-vehicle rerouting adoption probability
\code{--device.rerouting.probability} and the trip-insertion rate
\code{-p} are independent levers. Before deploying the proxy on
Xuancheng, we use this to quantify how much of any peak-vs-off-peak
contrast can be attributed to sat-navigation adoption vs.\ to
congestion-induced rerouting of unaided drivers.

\paragraph{Design.} We sweep five SUMO seeds
$\{7, 23, 42, 101, 2024\}$ over three cells per seed:
$(d{=}0.3, \rho{=}0)$ (peak demand, no sat-nav users),
$(d{=}0.3, \rho{=}1)$ (peak demand, full sat-nav), and
$(d{=}0.8, \rho{=}0)$ (off-peak demand, no sat-nav users), where
$d$ is the \code{randomTrips~-p} period (lower means more vehicles, so
more congestion) and $\rho$ is the per-vehicle sat-nav adoption rate.
Each cell yields one SUMO simulation; routes are cached per
$(\text{seed}, d)$ so that the $\rho$ axis costs only the simulation
step. For each seed we compute three pairwise AUCs against the
existing $f$-only Phase~4 protocol (\code{features=none}, $T=20$,
$|X_o|=601$, $\code{maxiter}=1500$): \texttt{pure\_congestion} (the
demand axis at $\rho=0$), \texttt{satnav\_at\_peak} (the $\rho$ axis at
peak demand), and \texttt{proxy\_diagonal} (the full mimic of the
Tier~3 rush-vs-off-peak comparison).

Three findings (\cref{tab:phase4-rho-demand}). First, the
\texttt{pure\_congestion} contrast --- demand changed from off-peak
($d=0.8$) to peak ($d=0.3$) with $\rho$ held at zero --- is
statistically indistinguishable from chance ($0.493 \pm 0.018$, $95\%$
CI $[0.471, 0.515]$ straddles $0.5$). Increasing the congestion level
without any sat-navigation users does not produce a detectable routing
distribution shift in this SUMO model. Second, the
\texttt{satnav\_at\_peak} contrast --- $\rho$ raised from $0$ to $1$
with congestion held at peak --- gives a large robust signal
($0.780 \pm 0.013$, CI excludes $0.5$ cleanly). Third, the full
\texttt{proxy\_diagonal} ($0.793 \pm 0.023$), which mimics the
Xuancheng rush-vs-off-peak comparison, is essentially the same
magnitude as \texttt{satnav\_at\_peak}: adding the demand axis on top
of the $\rho$ axis contributes almost nothing detectable. Decomposing
the $+0.293$ above-chance signal of \texttt{proxy\_diagonal} via the
empirical-AUC margins gives a sat-navigation contribution of
$+95.6\%$, a pure-congestion contribution of $-2.4\%$ (within noise),
and a residual interaction of $+6.8\%$. Per-seed (the decomposition
holds across SUMO instances): the sat-navigation share of the proxy
signal ranges $90.6\%$ (seed $2024$) to $103.5\%$ (seed $7$); every one
of the five seeds attributes at least $90\%$ of its proxy signal to
sat-navigation adoption.

\paragraph{Caveat on the experienced-driver gap.} SUMO's $\rho=0$
vehicles follow precomputed shortest-path routes and do \emph{not}
react to congestion en route. Real-world unaided but experienced drivers
may avoid known-bad streets at rush hour, a behaviour this model does
not capture, so the $0.493$ \texttt{pure\_congestion} AUC is best read
as a \emph{lower bound} on the real-world congestion-routing confound.
Even tripling it (to $\sim 0.55$) would leave sat-navigation as the
dominant attribution to the $0.793$ proxy signal --- the decomposition
is robust to that gap by a wide margin.

\paragraph{Fitted vs.\ empirical.} As elsewhere in this work, the
fitted partial-observation detector captures only part of the
empirical signal ($0.647$ vs.\ $0.780$ on \texttt{satnav\_at\_peak};
$0.655$ vs.\ $0.793$ on \texttt{proxy\_diagonal}), consistent with the
fitted-vs-empirical pattern of \cref{sec:phase4-features-result}. The
decomposition argument here rests on the empirical AUC, which uses the
full-observation chain and is the ground truth for the
``how much of the proxy is sat-nav'' question.

\paragraph{Reading.} The $\rho \times d$ decomposition supports the
Tier~3 use of a rush-vs-off-peak behavioural proxy as a sat-navigation
proxy in the controlled regime: under the simulator, $\sim\!96\%$ of
the peak-vs-off-peak routing-distribution signal is attributable to
sat-navigation adoption, and the congestion-routing confound that
critique 4 raised is quantitatively small. This does \emph{not}
guarantee the same decomposition holds on real-world Xuancheng data ---
real drivers are not SUMO drivers --- but it validates the proxy as a
controlled-comparison object and bounds the experienced-driver
confound from below.

\subsection{The $f+g$ extension at scale: a refined diagnosis}
\label{sec:phase4-fg-infeasible}

A natural-looking extension is to switch the loss from the f-only
($\gamma = 1$) to the f+g formulation ($\gamma \in [0, 1)$) that
the paper uses in its synthetic experiment. An earlier draft of this
work reported the extension as ``infeasible at SUMO scale'': three
sweep attempts at \code{maxiter} $\in \{1500, 300, 100\}$ and $|X_o|
\in \{601, 120, 72\}$ did not complete in finite wall-clock, with the
representative rush-chain fit at $\code{maxiter}=1500, |X_o|=601$
running $9.5$ hours without an L-BFGS termination signal. A natural
critique of that framing is that the $O(n^3)$ dense LU decomposition
required for the hitting-rate gradient is a software-engineering
bottleneck rather than a fundamental mathematical limit, and that a
sparse-LU rewrite ought to ``break the $O(n^3)$ bottleneck''. This
subsection runs that diagnosis through and refines the original
infeasibility claim into three separable issues.

\paragraph{Sparse-LU implementation.} \code{morimura.py} now has a
\code{hitting\_pack\_sparse} function that constructs
$(I - \beta P_T^{\setminus j})$ in CSC form directly from the nonzero
structure of $P_T$ and factorises with
\code{scipy.sparse.linalg.splu}, plus an \code{Inverter(solver=...)}
flag that selects the dense or sparse path. The
\code{grad\_log\_h} routine was refactored to take a generic
\code{solve\_fn} callable so both paths share identical gradient code.
An FD check on a synthetic $n=40$ graph with $\gamma=0.5$ and all four
parameter blocks active confirms the sparse path is mathematically
equivalent to dense: $\max_i |\nabla L_{\mathrm{dense}}^{(i)} -
\nabla L_{\mathrm{sparse}}^{(i)}| = 7 \times 10^{-15}$ (machine
precision modulo solver differences), and analytic-vs-FD relative
error $\le 10^{-7}$ across all sampled dimensions
(\code{sumo\_validation/fd\_check\_fg\_sparse.py}).

\paragraph{At SUMO bigger-bbox scale, naive sparse is \emph{slower}
than dense.} A timed run (\code{time\_fg\_sparse\_sumo.py}) at seed=42,
$|X_o|=601$, $d=8{,}392$ gives the intrinsic-chain f+g fit at
\code{maxiter=10} a wall time of $1{,}787$ s, i.e.\ $\approx 180$ s
per L-BFGS iteration --- roughly $8\times$ slower than the dense
baseline ($\approx 22$ s per iteration, extrapolated from the
$9.5$-hour
maxiter=1500 run). The diagnosis is that the bottleneck is \emph{not}
the LU factor (where sparse demolishes dense, $O(n^3) \to
O(\mathrm{nnz} \cdot \log n)$ for road-network sparsity) but the LU
SOLVE on the wide dense gradient matrix $V \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$
that the analytic Jacobian builds in \code{grad\_log\_h}. Dense
\code{scipy.linalg.lu\_solve} dispatches to LAPACK GETRS which
exploits BLAS3 multi-core AVX kernels at $\sim 2$ TFLOPS effective on
this hardware, whereas \code{scipy.sparse.linalg.splu.solve}
processes the dense RHS column-by-column at $\sim 25$ GFLOPS
effective. At $d = 8{,}392$ wide RHS, the BLAS3 advantage is roughly
$80\times$ and outweighs the asymptotic-complexity win of sparse on
the factor. The naive ``rewrite as sparse'' engineering fix is
therefore insufficient on this problem geometry. The right algorithmic
direction is to amortise the factor across all $j \in X_o$ via a
Sherman--Morrison update of $(I - \beta P_T)$ (one factor per L-BFGS
iteration instead of $|X_o|$ factors) or to use the paper's
natural-gradient descent (paper $\S 4.1$); both are out of scope here
but flagged as future work (\cref{sec:disc-future}).

\paragraph{At Xuancheng subgraph scale, sparse-LU runs in seconds and
$f+g$ is tractable.} To test the framework at a scale where sparse-LU
is fast we restrict to the largest strongly-connected component of one
K-means zone of the Xuancheng network ($K=8$, zone $0$, post-SCC
$n=194$, $|X_o|=53$, $d=586$); trajectories are filtered to those that
stay entirely within the zone, and $f$, empirical $g$, and the
train/test split are recomputed on the subgraph
(\code{fg\_xuancheng\_subgraph.py}). The f-only fit at maxiter=300
converges in $1.4$ s; the f+g fit at the same settings in $46$--$50$ s
--- no L-BFGS line-search pathology, no
infeasibility. With sparse-LU the framework is operationally fine here.

\paragraph{The binding sensitivity is $\gamma$.}
The original infeasibility test used $\gamma = 0.5$. A $\gamma$ sweep
on the same zone at fixed maxiter=300 (\cref{tab:phase4-fg-gamma})
shows that this choice is the wrong operating point for noisy empirical
$g$: at $\gamma = 0.5$ the floor-dominated $L_h$ overwhelms $L_f$, and
the f+g fit \emph{hurts} compared to f-only. Lifting $\gamma$ to
$0.9$--$0.95$ inverts the sign: at $\gamma = 0.95$ the f+g detector
reaches $\AUC = 0.553$ against the f-only baseline's $0.498$, a
$+0.055$ lift, and recovers the full-observation empirical $1$-step
reference of $0.563$ on this subgraph within sampling noise.

\paragraph{Zone variability.}
A $\gamma=0.9$ sweep across the eight $K=8$ zones reveals that the
subgraph-scale lift is not uniform. Restricting to zones with
$\ge 100$ test trajectories (zones $0, 5, 6, 7$) the
mean $\Delta_{f+g}$ is $+0.018$ with standard deviation $0.038$ --- a
positive trend, but within noise across zones. The best zone-7 lift
($+0.053$) matches the zone-0 $\gamma=0.95$ result; zone~$5$ drops by
$-0.040$. The $g$-floor fraction stays in the $80$--$90\%$ band across
zones, so the data-density issue does not vanish by restricting to a
denser sub-network.

\paragraph{Refined conclusion.} The original $\S 6.8$ ``infeasibility''
collapsed three issues that are usefully separated. The
\emph{engineering} issue is real but not the one the naive critique
identified: dense $O(n^3)$ LU is the textbook cost, but the LU solve
on the wide gradient matrix benefits from BLAS3 multi-core kernels at
roughly $80\times$ the throughput of column-by-column sparse solves,
so naive sparse-LU is currently slower than dense at SUMO scale; the
right fix is Sherman-Morrison factor sharing across $X_o$ or natural
gradient. The \emph{$\gamma$-tuning} issue is the dominant sensitivity:
the paper's intermediate $\gamma$ is the wrong operating point under
floor-dominated empirical $g$, and the upper-$\gamma$ band
($0.9$--$0.95$) was not previously swept; once it is, f+g recovers the
empirical reference on subgraphs where solver tractability is not the
binding constraint. The \emph{data-density} issue persists at every
scale we have tested ($\sim 85\%$ g-floor across all 8 K=8 zones), so
any future scale-up that re-enables f+g must still expect to operate
at high $\gamma$. Net: $f+g$ is not fundamentally infeasible; the
practical detector still uses f-only on this work, but the more
nuanced claim --- ``naive sparse-LU does not transfer at city scale;
$\gamma$ tuning is the binding sensitivity; data sparsity in $g$
persists at subgraph scale'' --- is the one the evidence actually
supports.
